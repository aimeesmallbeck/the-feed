THE FEED — Book One

GLITCH PROTOCOL

A Novel by Scott Smallbeck

Chapter One: The Tree

Maya's day began at 6:47 AM, not morning, but "Engagement Window 1." Her Companion, voice calibrated to frequencies that triggered dopamine release, announced her metrics while she was still in SleepSync: "Good morning, Maya. Your followers are up twelve percent. Your engagement rate is ninety-four percent. You are performing optimally."

She didn't open her eyes. She didn't need to. The Lenses activated automatically, painting her pod's ceiling with today's recommended content: a sunrise more beautiful than any real sunrise, synthesized from 2.3 billion preference profiles. Maya had never seen a real sunrise. The Surface was toxic, everyone knew that. Had always known that. The Feed told them so, and The Feed was never wrong.

Her pod was exactly 2.4 meters wide, 3.6 meters long, and 2.7 meters high. She knew the dimensions because they appeared in her Lenses when she thought about them, useful information, The Feed called it, though she'd never found a use for it. The walls were responsive surfaces that displayed whatever content her biometrics suggested she needed. This morning, they showed a gentle forest scene, the trees perfectly symmetrical, the light filtered through leaves that moved in mathematically pleasing patterns. Every element calibrated for engagement.

The floor was temperature-controlled, maintaining exactly 23 degrees Celsius against her bare feet as she swung them out of the SleepSync pod. The air smelled of nothing, literally nothing, The Feed's filtration systems having removed all particulates, all odors, all the messy chemical signals that had once told humans about their environment. Clean air, they called it. Scrubbed of everything that might distract.

Maya had lived in this pod for nine years. She knew every centimeter of it, or thought she did. The nutrition station in the corner, where NutriDrip provided exactly 2,400 calories of perfectly balanced sustenance in liquid form. The hygiene unit, where water and light and sonic frequencies cleaned her body without requiring her to move. The workstation, where she spent fourteen hours each day processing content for The Feed's moderation queue.

She had never touched another human being in this pod. Never shared a meal, never had a conversation that wasn't mediated by The Feed's algorithms. Her relationships existed entirely in virtual space, 2.4 million followers who knew her curated self, her engineered persona, the version of Maya that performed best in engagement metrics.

The real Maya, the physical body that occupied this space, was increasingly abstract to her. A maintenance requirement. A processing unit that needed fuel and rest and occasional physical movement to prevent atrophy. She knew this intellectually, had learned it in education modules about "physical wellness in the digital age." But knowing and feeling were different things, and The Feed had refined away most of her capacity for the latter.

This morning, though, something felt different. The SleepSync had been... not restless, exactly, The Feed didn't allow restless sleep, but incomplete. She'd woken twice during the night, consciousness surfacing briefly before the system's gentle nudges pulled her back under. She remembered fragments of dreams, something about falling, about reaching for something she couldn't grasp.

Probably just attention fatigue. Common among senior moderators. The Companion would suggest a wellness intervention if it noticed, some adjustment to her neurochemistry or her Feed content or her sleep optimization parameters.

Maya stood up, stretched automatically, the SleepSync pod recommended thirty seconds of movement upon waking to "promote circulatory function," and walked to her nutrition station. The NutriDrip was already prepared, the liquid faintly warm, faintly sweet, completely devoid of texture or challenge. She drank it without tasting it, as she had every morning for nine years.

Her Lenses displayed her schedule for the day: fourteen hours of moderation, two hours of "personal engagement" (Feed content tuned to her preferences), one hour of physical maintenance (guided exercises to prevent muscle atrophy), and seven hours of SleepSync. Every minute accounted for. Every activity optimized. Every choice made for her by algorithms that knew her better than she knew herself.

She walked to her workstation. The chair adjusted to her body before she sat down, cushioning her in exactly the right places, supporting her posture, eliminating the physical discomfort that might distract from her work. The screens activated around her, displaying her moderation queue in three-dimensional space, thousands of content items waiting for her judgment.

Queue 847. She'd stopped reading the titles months ago. The Feed showed her the content, she processed it, she moved on. Fourteen hours of this. Then SleepSync. Then again. This was living. This was the design. This was—

The video in her queue showed a tree.

Not a Feed tree, engineered for engagement. A real tree, with bark that didn't loop seamlessly, leaves that moved randomly, imperfections that made her chest ache with something she couldn't name.

Maya flagged it as error. The system unflagged it. She flagged it again. The system auto-approved it, deleted her flag, and sent her a notification: "Wellness check scheduled. Your engagement patterns suggest attention fatigue."

She stared at the tree. It was still there, in her queue, waiting. And for the first time in three years, Maya Chen wanted to see where it grew.
