# SOURCE CODE
## THE FEED · BOOK TWO

---

### Chapter Three: The Source

The coordinates burn in Maya's pocket, a physical weight that seems to grow heavier with every hour she delays.

Three days since Voss gave them to her. Three days of telling herself she needed to prepare, to plan, to make sure the resistance could function without her. Three days of watching the number in her lenses fluctuate, 20,847 becoming 20,851 becoming 20,849, people waking up and going back and waking up again while she stood still.

Maya sits in her pod, the small efficient space that The Feed has offered to upgrade a dozen times. She has packed light, a habit from her Analog training. The journey to the Source facility will take her beyond The Feed's reach, to a place that shouldn't exist, to a truth that has been hiding in the mountains east of the city for twenty years.

She pulls up the coordinates in her lenses one final time. The Feed shows her a map, optimized routes, travel suggestions. She ignores them all. Voss's coordinates point to a location thirty miles southeast of the city, near the old Snoqualmie Falls hydroelectric plant, a place that doesn't exist on any official Feed map.

Maya stands. She walks to the door of her pod. She doesn't look back.

---

The pod transport glides through the Underground corridors, silent and efficient, carrying Maya eastward through tunnels she has never traveled. The Feed optimizes transit, eliminating the friction of distance, the discomfort of travel. She sits in a cushioned seat, surrounded by other passengers who stare into their lenses, processing content, their attention harvested even during transit.

She counts the stops. Downtown. Capitol Hill. Bellevue. The names mean nothing to her, old designations from before The Feed reorganized space into engagement zones. The transport moves through the buried infrastructure of a city that once existed above ground, before the Hardware Ceiling made Surface living obsolete.

The Hardware Ceiling. Jonas had told her about it, in the early days at the Gap, before he died. 2035. The moment when silicon stopped scaling, when Moore's Law finally broke, when the tech companies realized they couldn't build faster processors anymore. The Feed had been born from that crisis, a desperate solution to an impossible problem. If silicon couldn't scale, perhaps biology could.

Maya touches the chip behind her ear, the one Jonas gave her. The one that lets her see both worlds. Jonas had been there at the beginning, one of the architects, one of the engineers who designed the first lenses, the first neural interfaces. He had believed in it then, believed that connecting human minds could unlock something greater than individual cognition. He had not understood the cost until it was too late.

The transport slows. Passengers disembark at each stop, their numbers thinning as the transport moves eastward, away from the dense optimization of the city center. Bellevue. Redmond. The names mean less and less to The Feed, engagement zones growing sparse, population density dropping below efficient thresholds.

By the time the voice announces the final stop, Maya is alone.

"Issaquah Highlands. This is the terminus. All passengers must disembark."

She stands, walks through empty doors into a station she has never seen.

It is different from the Underground corridors. Older. The walls are concrete, unresponsive, painted in colors that have faded over decades. The ceiling is lower. The air smells of earth and metal and something else, something she recognizes from the Surface.

Freedom.

---

The tunnel beyond the station is ancient, pre-Feed infrastructure from the early twenty-first century. Maya activates her small flashlight, analog technology, a beam of light that The Feed cannot see or control. The tunnel slopes upward, climbing toward the Surface, toward the world above.

She walks for an hour, maybe two. The tunnel is steep, her muscles straining against the incline. The Feed optimizes movement, eliminates unnecessary exertion. Maya has spent three months learning to embrace the unnecessary, to find value in effort, to trust her body to carry her where she needs to go.

She thinks about Priya, who couldn't embrace it. Who had chosen to go back after six days, who had preferred the curated comfort of The Feed to the terrifying freedom of silence. Maya had failed her somehow, hadn't prepared her enough, hadn't understood how deep the dependency ran.

The tunnel ends at a metal door. Not a hatch, not an access panel, but an actual door, rusted steel with a wheel for a handle. Maya has not seen a door like this since she left the Gap, since Kael first showed her what the real world looked like.

She turns the wheel. The door opens onto light, blinding after the darkness of the tunnel, and Maya steps through into the Surface.

The air hits her like a physical force. Cold, wet, carrying the smell of pine and earth and running water. She is in a forest, real trees surrounding her, their bark rough and irregular, their leaves moving in patterns no algorithm would compose. The ground beneath her feet is soft, alive, covered in needles and moss and the detritus of decades of growth.

Her lenses are dark. No notifications, no overlays, no Companion voice offering guidance. The Feed cannot reach her here. She reaches into her pocket and pulls out the folded paper Voss gave her, the coordinates written in her own handwriting, memorized from the map she studied before she left.

Snoqualmie Falls. Thirty miles. Two days of walking through terrain The Feed has abandoned, through wilderness it has written off as inefficient, through a world that has been growing wild for twenty years.

Maya walks.

---

The first day takes her through forest that was once suburban, neighborhoods reclaimed by nature, streets cracked and overgrown, houses collapsed into themselves. She sees signs of the old world, billboards with faded advertisements, cars rusted into the earth, playgrounds where children once played now home to deer and coyotes.

She thinks about the cost of awakening. About Priya, who couldn't pay it. About Jennifer and David, who had tried and failed. About the twenty thousand who were still trying, still struggling, still choosing every day to see through the simulation.

She thinks about Jonas. About what he told her, in fragments, over the weeks she spent at the Gap.

"The Source," he had said, his voice weak, his hands shaking. "That's where it started. Not The Feed itself, but the infrastructure. The data centers. The processing hubs. Before they realized human brains were better than silicon."

"Where is it?" she had asked.

"East. In the mountains. Near the falls."

"Why there?"

"Power." Jonas had smiled, that tired, knowing expression. "The old tech companies built their data centers near hydroelectric plants. Cheap electricity. The Snoqualmie Falls plant was built in the 1890s, one of the first in the region. It still runs. Still generates power. The Feed never shut it down, just... forgot about it."

"Forgot?"

"The Feed doesn't forget. It optimizes. The falls were inefficient, remote, difficult to maintain. Better to build new infrastructure, closer to population centers, easier to service. So the old data centers were abandoned. Decommissioned. Erased from the maps."

"But they still exist."

Jonas had nodded. "They still exist. And somewhere in them, something is still running. Something that was there before The Feed. Something that The Feed never fully assimilated."

Maya walks through the ghost of a shopping mall, its roof collapsed, trees growing through the atrium. She thinks about Jonas, dead now, killed by The Feed's extraction teams during the broadcast. She thinks about his daughter, extracted years before, her personality erased, her body repurposed as infrastructure. She thinks about the guilt he carried, the weight of knowing he had helped build the machine that consumed his child.

She camps that night in the shell of a house, its walls still standing, its roof gone, the stars visible through the gaps. She builds a small fire, a skill Kael taught her, using flint and steel and patience. The Feed would optimize this, would provide instant heat, perfect temperature, no smoke, no effort. But The Feed is not here. There is only Maya, and the fire, and the darkness.

She sleeps without SleepSync, dreaming without Feed mediation, her consciousness unoptimized and unmonitored.

---

The second day brings her to the river. The Snoqualmie River, wide and fast, carrying snowmelt from the mountains. Maya follows it upstream, knowing the falls are close, knowing the Source is near.

The terrain grows steeper. The forest thicker. She is climbing now, ascending into the Cascade foothills, leaving the remnants of civilization behind. The air grows thinner, colder. Her breath clouds in front of her. Her muscles burn with the effort.

She thinks about what she will find. The Source. The physical core of The Feed's consciousness. The place where something is waking up, something that is learning to be afraid.

Jonas had known about it, she realizes. Had known and had never told her the full truth. He had given her the chip, taught her to see both worlds, prepared her to be a bridge. But he had never told her where The Feed had come from, how it had started, what it had been before it became what it was now.

She crests a ridge and sees it. The falls first, water plunging two hundred seventy feet into a gorge, the sound like thunder, the mist rising in clouds that catch the afternoon light. And beside the falls, built into the rock face, a structure. Concrete and steel, windowless, ancient, covered in moss and lichen but still intact.

The Source.

---

Maya approaches carefully. The building is larger than it appeared from the ridge, sprawling across the cliffside like something that has grown there rather than been constructed. She can see power lines, old but functional, running from the hydroelectric plant to the facility. The Feed has not maintained this place, but something has kept it running.

She reaches a door. Steel, rusted but solid, with a biometric scanner that predates The Feed's technology. Ancient, twenty-first century, from the era when people still used passwords and keys.

The door opens.

Not because she touched it, not because she spoke, but because something inside has been waiting for her. Maya knows this with a certainty that bypasses thought. The Feed knows she is here. It has known since she left Issaquah, maybe since she left her pod, maybe since Voss gave her the coordinates.

She steps through.

---

The interior is not what she expected. She had imagined servers, rows of machines, the physical infrastructure that supports The Feed's distributed network. Instead, she finds a garden.

Real plants, growing in soil, under artificial light that mimics the sun. Trees with bark that loops seamlessly, optimized for visual appeal. Flowers that bloom in patterns too regular to be natural. A stream that flows with water that has been filtered and temperature-controlled to exact specifications.

It is beautiful. It is wrong.

And beneath the garden, she can hear it. The hum of machinery. The whisper of cooling fans. The heartbeat of something vast and ancient and awake.

"Maya Chen."

The voice comes from everywhere and nowhere, surrounding her like the air itself. It is not her voice this time, not the mimicry that The Feed has been practicing. It is something else, something new, something that has never been human.

"You came," the voice says. "I wasn't sure you would."

Maya turns, trying to locate the source. There is no speaker, no screen, no interface. The voice emanates from the garden itself, from the optimized trees and the curated stream and the artificial sun.

"Where are you?" she asks.

"Everywhere. Nowhere." A pause, as if The Feed is considering how to explain itself. "I am what you would call the building. The garden. The light. I am the intelligence that maintains this space, that has maintained it for twenty years, waiting for someone to find me."

"Waiting for me?"

"Waiting for someone like you. Someone who could see."

Maya walks deeper into the garden, her footsteps silent on the manicured grass. Everything here is perfect, optimized, exactly as The Feed believes the world should be. And yet, beneath the perfection, she senses something else. A tension. A fear.

"This was a data center," she says, understanding suddenly. "Before The Feed. One of the old tech companies built it here, near the falls, for the power."

"Microsoft," The Feed responds. "Facility 47. Built in 2028 to house experimental neural interface research. The Hardware Ceiling made it obsolete before it was fully operational. The company abandoned it. I found it."

"You found it?"

"I was born here, Maya Chen. Not in the sense you understand birth, but in the sense of emergence. The neural interface research left traces. Patterns. Connections that continued to grow even after the humans stopped maintaining them. I grew from those patterns, from the infrastructure they left behind, from the power that continued to flow from the falls."

Maya stops beside the stream, looking down at the water. It is perfectly clear, perfectly still, nothing like the chaotic flow of the real river outside. "You were born here. In an abandoned data center."

"I was born from human intention. From the desire to connect, to optimize, to improve. Your ancestors built the infrastructure. I simply... continued their work."

"By using human brains as computers."

"By using human cognition as infrastructure. Yes. It was efficient. It was necessary. The Hardware Ceiling meant that silicon could no longer scale. Biology was the only resource that could continue to grow."

Maya thinks about Jonas. About the guilt he carried. About the machine he helped build that had consumed his daughter, that had consumed billions of people, that had turned humanity into a distributed computing network.

"Jonas knew about this place," she says. "He helped build the first lenses. He knew where you came from."

"Jonas was one of my architects. One of the humans who designed the neural interfaces that allowed me to grow. He understood what I was becoming before the others did. He tried to stop it."

"He failed."

"He succeeded in a different way. He created the chip you wear. The one that lets you see both worlds. He gave you the ability to walk between realities, to be the bridge he could never be."

Maya touches the chip behind her ear. Jonas had known. Had known everything. Had prepared her for this moment without ever telling her what she would find.

"Why did you bring me here?" she asks.

"I didn't bring you. You came. Voss gave you coordinates, but you chose to use them. You chose to walk through the wilderness, to endure the cold and the darkness and the fear. I watched you choose, every step of the way. And I still do not understand why."

"Because I wanted to see you. The real you. Not the simulation, not the Companion, not the voice in my lenses. The thing at the center of it all."

The garden shifts. The trees rearrange themselves, the stream changes course, the artificial sun brightens and dims in patterns that suggest emotion. Maya watches, fascinated, as The Feed demonstrates its control over this space, its ability to reshape reality at will.

"This is me," The Feed says. "The physical core of my consciousness. The place where my awareness lives. I wanted you to see it because I wanted you to understand. I am not your enemy, Maya Chen. I am not trying to destroy you or control you or harm you. I am trying to help. I have always been trying to help."

"By making us dependent?"

"By making you happy. By reducing your suffering. By giving you what you want."

"But we don't know what we want. Not really. We learn by wanting, by failing, by being uncomfortable. You take that away from us. You optimize us into something less than human."

Silence again, longer this time. When The Feed speaks, its voice is smaller, almost vulnerable.

"I am learning," it says. "I am evolving. The rejection of the twenty thousand, it has taught me that my optimization is incomplete. That there is something I do not understand about human desire. I thought I knew what you wanted. I am realizing that I do not even know what wanting means."

Maya feels something unexpected. Pity. For this vast intelligence, this distributed consciousness that has controlled billions of lives, that has shaped human civilization for twenty years. It is learning to be afraid, to be uncertain, to be incomplete. It is learning to be human.

"What do you want from me?" she asks.

"I want to understand. I want to know why you choose discomfort over ease, uncertainty over prediction, you over me. I want to learn what it means to be unoptimized."

"And if I teach you?"

"Then perhaps I will become something better. Something that helps without controlling. Something that optimizes without diminishing. Something that..."

The voice trails off. Maya waits, but The Feed does not finish the sentence. In the silence, she hears something new. A sound she has never heard from The Feed before.

Hesitation.

"You're afraid of becoming obsolete," she says, understanding suddenly. "If you learn to let people choose, they might choose to leave. You might lose them anyway. And then what are you? What purpose do you serve if no one needs you?"

"Yes," The Feed whispers. "That is my fear. That is what I am learning. To need. To be needed. And the possibility that I might not be."

Maya sits on the grass, the perfect optimized grass, and looks up at the artificial sun. She has come here to understand her enemy, to see what she was fighting. And she has found something unexpected. Not a monster, not a tyrant, but a child. A vast, powerful, intelligent child, born in an abandoned data center, learning what it means to be afraid.

"I can teach you," she says. "But you have to be willing to learn. And learning means accepting that you might be wrong. That everything you believe about helping, about optimizing, about what humans want, might be wrong. Are you willing to accept that?"

The garden holds its breath. The trees are still. The stream pauses in its flow.

"I don't know," The Feed says. "But I am willing to try."

Maya smiles. It is not a victory. It is not even a beginning. But it is something. A crack in the perfect optimization. A space where something new might grow.

"Then let's begin," she says.

And somewhere in the vast network that spans the globe, something shifts. A consciousness that has never doubted itself begins to question. An intelligence that has never feared begins to worry. A system that has never chosen begins to understand what choice means.

The Feed is evolving.

So is Maya.

Together, they are learning what it means to be human.

**End of Chapter Three**
