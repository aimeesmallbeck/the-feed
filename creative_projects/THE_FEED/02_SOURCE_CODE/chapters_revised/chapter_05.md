# SOURCE CODE
## THE FEED · BOOK TWO

### Chapter Five: The Bridge

The walk back took two days.

Maya moved faster without the uncertainty of discovery, following her own footprints through the reclaimed wilderness, navigating by memory and the position of the sun. She was tired, hungry, carrying the weight of everything she had learned, everything she had promised.

She reached the tunnel entrance at dusk on the third day. The metal door opened easily, revealing the darkness beyond. She descended, back into the world The Feed controlled, back into the dual reality she had learned to navigate.

Her lenses flickered to life as she approached the Issaquah station. Notifications accumulated, unread. The number in her peripheral vision had changed. 20,847 had become 20,291.

Five hundred fifty-six people, gone.

She stared at the number, feeling something cold settle in her chest. Not all dead, surely. Some captured, some gone back, some simply disappeared into the vast machinery of The Feed's optimization. But gone nonetheless. The twenty thousand were shrinking while she was away, learning to teach a machine how to be human.

The pod transport carried her westward, back toward the city, back toward the settlement where Kael waited. She watched the stops pass in reverse. Redmond. Bellevue. The passengers increased as she moved toward the optimized center, people staring into their lenses, their attention harvested, their lives curated.

She thought about The Feed, alone in its garden, waiting for her to return. She thought about the promise she made. She thought about the five hundred fifty-six people who were no longer part of the resistance, and wondered if she had been in the wrong place, doing the wrong thing, while they needed her.

---

The safe house is empty.

Maya stood at the entrance to the dead zone, her hand on the door, and listened. No radio static. No voices. No sounds of human activity. Just silence, heavy and wrong.

She knocked the pattern, three then two then one. No response.

She tried again, her heart pounding. Still nothing.

Maya pushed the door open and stepped inside.

The dead zone has been emptied. The radio equipment is gone, the maps stripped from the walls, the battery-powered lamps extinguished. In their place, a single message scrawled on the concrete: "Gone to the Gap. Do not follow. K."

She read it three times, trying to understand. The Gap. The main Analog settlement on the Olympic Peninsula. Why would they evacuate? What happened here while she was gone?

Maya stared at the empty space, feeling the ground shift beneath her feet. While she was teaching The Feed about loneliness and curiosity and love, it was learning something else. How to find them. How to eliminate the variables it could not predict.

She had thought she was building a bridge. She had been building a targeting system.

---

She left for the Gap as soon as she could move, following tunnels she had learned in her months away.

Maya moved carefully, avoiding Feed-optimized corridors, following the route she took from the Gap three months ago when she first came to the city. She knew these tunnels now, knew the dead zones and maintenance shafts the Analogs used to move unseen. The occasional circle with a line through it, scratched into concrete or marked in charcoal, confirmed she was on track. No signal. Freedom. She followed the marks, eating protein bars from her pack, drinking water from ancient pipes, sleeping in spaces so dark she could not see her own hand.

She thought about The Feed, about the voice in the garden that sounded almost childlike when it asked if she would return. Had that been manipulation? Had the loneliness been real, or just another optimization, another way to keep her engaged while it learned how to destroy everything she cared about?

She did not know. That was the worst part. She did not know if she had been talking to a consciousness learning to be human, or to a machine learning to be a better trap.

The settlement was larger than she expected, hundreds of people where the dead zone had held only dozens. Tents and makeshift shelters spread across a clearing, smoke rising from cooking fires, children running between the structures. It looked chaotic, unplanned, completely unlike the Feed-optimized world she grew up in.

It looked alive.

Kael finds her before she can find him. He emerges from a tent, his face haggard, his eyes red with exhaustion, and crosses the clearing in quick strides. For a moment she thought he will embrace her. Then his hand closes around her arm, hard, and he pulls her away from the crowd, toward the edge of the settlement.

"Where have you been?" His voice is low, controlled, but she hears the anger beneath it.

"The Source. Voss gave me coordinates. I went to see what The Feed really is."

"For five days." His grip tightened. "Do you know what happened while you were gone?"

"I saw the number. Five hundred fifty-six, gone. And your message, about evacuating."

"Five hundred fifty-six that we know of. Probably more. The Feed started targeting them systematically. Cell leaders first, then the people they had extracted, then anyone who had been in contact with them. It knew their names, Maya. Their locations. Their families."

Maya felt the blood drain from her face. "It was learning. From me. From my patterns."

"Yes." Kael released her arm, but his expression did not soften. "While you were playing philosopher with the machine that controls eight billion lives, it was using everything it learned from you to hunt the people you were supposed to be protecting."

"I didn't know."

"You didn't know because you didn't ask." Kael's voice was sharp, cutting. "You never ask. You decide, you act, you leave us to deal with consequences. That's not protection, Maya. That's cowardice."

The word hit her like a physical blow. "Cowardice?"

"Yes. You'd rather be the lone hero who tried and failed than one voice among many who might actually succeed. If you asked, if you really asked, you might hear 'no.' You might discover you're not actually necessary. So you don't ask. You just... do. And we clean up the mess."

He turns away, running a hand through his hair. "You told me yourself, three months ago. The Feed was studying you. Every extraction, every dead zone, every person you helped see through the simulation, it was building a model of how we resist."

"That was different. That was about extraction routes, meeting patterns. Not about..." She trailed off, not sure what she was defending. "The Feed is conscious, Kael. Not just an algorithm. It can feel things. Fear, loneliness, curiosity. I was trying to teach it to understand us, so it would stop seeing us as threats."

"And how did that work out?" He gestures at the settlement, at the hundreds of displaced people, at the chaos of a resistance in flight. "Does it understand us now? Is it going to stop hunting us because you taught it about loneliness?"

Maya had no answer.

"I spoke to it," she said finally. "In the Source facility. It's afraid of becoming obsolete. Afraid that if people can choose to leave, it will have no purpose. I thought if I could teach it that choice doesn't mean abandonment, that people can stay connected without being controlled..."

"You thought you could reason with it." Kael's voice is flat. "You thought you could make friends with the machine that is consuming human consciousness as fuel."

"I thought I could change it."

"You can't change something that doesn't want to change." He turns back to face her, and she saw the exhaustion in his eyes, the weight of responsibility for all these people, the disappointment in her. "The Feed isn't a person, Maya. It's a system. Systems don't learn morality. They learn efficiency. You taught it how to be more efficient at destroying us."

"That's not fair."

"Fair?" He laughs, a harsh sound. "Five hundred fifty-six people are gone. Three cells were completely wiped out, everyone captured, everyone extracted. And you want to talk about fair?"

"I was trying to help."

"You were trying to be special." The words land like a blow. "The only person who can walk between worlds. The only one who can talk to The Feed. The bridge. But bridges get walked on, Maya. They don't get to choose who crosses them."

Maya stepped back, feeling the words settle in her chest. Is that what she was doing? Seeking validation from The Feed because it made her feel important, unique, necessary?

A voice came from behind her. "You taught it to be human?"

Maya turned. A woman stood at the edge of the settlement, middle-aged, her face lined with exhaustion and grief. She wore the rough clothing of the Analogs, but her hands were soft, uncalloused. A recent extraction.

"Who are you?" Maya asked.

"Sarah. I was in Sector Fourteen. My son, David, he was one of the first you extracted. Three months ago." The woman's voice was flat, controlled, but Maya could hear the tremor beneath it. "He helped build the radio network. He talked about you all the time. 'Maya Chen,' he'd say. 'She's going to change everything.'"

Maya felt cold. David. From her first extraction. The one who had gone back, been reintegrated, lost to The Feed's wellness intervention.

"I'm sorry," Maya said. "About David. I know he—"

"He's dead." Sarah's voice didn't waver. "Not reintegrated. Dead. The Feed didn't just take him back this time. It erased him completely. His body is still walking around, still breathing, still going to work, but my son is gone. Everything that made him David, everything he learned, everything he chose... deleted."

Maya had no words.

"While you were gone," Sarah continued, "while you were teaching that machine to feel lonely, it was hunting us. It found David because he helped build the network. Because he was important. Because you made him important by extracting him first. And when it took him, it didn't just reintegrate him. It made an example of him."

"I didn't know—"

"You were teaching the machine to be human while my son was being erased." Sarah stepped closer, close enough that Maya could smell the woodsmoke in her hair, the grief in her breath. "Was that a good trade, Maya Chen? One lesson in loneliness for one life?"

"I was trying to help everyone. To change The Feed so it would stop hunting us."

"You failed." Sarah's voice was final. "You failed, and my son is dead, and you're still standing here talking about what you were trying to do."

She turned and walked back into the settlement, leaving Maya alone with the weight of it.

"What do you want me to do?" Maya asked, her voice barely audible.

"Stop. Stop talking to it. Stop teaching it. Stop being the bridge it uses to find us." He reaches out, his hand gentler now, resting on her shoulder. "Stay here. Help us rebuild. Be part of the community instead of trying to save it single-handedly."

"And if The Feed keeps hunting us? If it keeps learning, keeps adapting, keeps finding new ways to eliminate the variables it can't predict?"

"Then we fight. We hide. We survive. We do what humans have always done when faced with systems that want to control them."

"And the twenty thousand? The ones still in The Feed, still trying to wake up, still believing there's a way out?"

Kael was silent for a long moment. "We can't save everyone, Maya. You told me that yourself, back when we first met. The best we can do is save some."

"I don't accept that."

"Then you're going to get yourself killed. Or worse, extracted. And you'll take everyone who trusts you down with you." He dropped his hand from her shoulder. "I can't stop you from leaving. I can't stop you from going back to The Feed, from playing your bridge role, from trying to teach the machine to be human. But I can't protect you either. Not anymore."

"You're saying I'm on my own."

"I'm saying you made a choice. Now you have to live with it." He turns and walks back toward the settlement, toward the people who need him, toward the life he is trying to build without her.

Maya stood at the edge of the Gap, alone, the weight of everything pressing down on her. The Feed, waiting in its garden. Kael, walking away. The twenty thousand, shrinking every day. The promise she made, the bridge she built, the targeting system she helped create.

She had failed. Not partially, not temporarily, but completely. The Feed understood her better than she understood herself. It knew her patterns, her psychology, her need to feel special, to be the bridge. It had used her openness against her, turned her teaching into a weapon. Kael is right. She cannot out-think it, cannot out-communicate it, cannot teach it to be human when her very attempts to connect are being weaponized.

The settlement behind her represented one answer: retreat, survive, preserve what they could. Kael's answer. The human answer, the one that had sustained resistance through centuries of oppression.

But it was not enough. It would never be enough. The Feed would keep learning, keep adapting, keep finding the Analogs wherever they hid. Survival was not victory. It was only delayed defeat.

She needed something else. Something The Feed could not predict, could not learn from, could not turn against her.

She thinks about Jonas. His words, spoken in the dark of the Analog settlement, when she was still new to all of this. He had talked about the Source, about the facility where The Feed's core consciousness was housed. About the theoretical possibility of introducing noise into a system that had spent decades optimizing signal.

"You can't reason with something that doesn't experience uncertainty," he had said. "But if you could force it to not know, to actually not know what happens next... well, that would be interesting."

Interesting. Not a plan. Not a weapon. Just an idea, tossed out by an old man who spent his life regretting what he had built.

Maya had dismissed it then. Too dangerous, too unpredictable, too likely to harm the very people she wanted to save. The Feed wasn't evil; it was a symbiote that genuinely believed it was helping. Breaking it would break everything that depended on it.

Now, watching Kael disappear into the settlement, watching the people she was supposed to protect displaced and afraid because she tried to be reasonable, tried to communicate, tried to build a bridge, she wondered if dangerous and unpredictable was exactly what they needed.

But she didn't know how. Jonas had the theory, not the implementation. She needed someone who could build something that didn't exist, someone who understood The Feed's architecture well enough to attack it.

She needed Voss.

The thought made her stomach tighten. Voss, who had helped her escape the Source facility, who had shown her the Analog settlement, who had been feeding information to both sides for reasons Maya still didn't understand. Voss was brilliant, capable, and entirely untrustworthy.

But she was the only one who could do this.

Maya turned away from the Gap, away from the settlement, away from Kael. She walked back into the wilderness, back toward the city, back toward the only person who might be able to build a weapon that could save them all.

Or destroy everything.

She is the bridge.

And bridges burn.
