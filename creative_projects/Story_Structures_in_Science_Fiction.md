# Story Structures in Science Fiction

*A guide to narrative frameworks with sci-fi examples*

---

## 1. THREE-ACT STRUCTURE

**The classic:** Setup → Confrontation → Resolution

**Act I (Setup):** Establish world, character, status quo. Inciting incident. First plot point (point of no return).

**Act II (Confrontation):** Rising action. Complications. Midpoint (shift in understanding). Things get worse. Dark moment.

**Act III (Resolution):** Climax. Resolution. New normal.

**Why it works:** Familiar, satisfying, mirrors how humans process change.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **The Matrix** — Neo learns the truth (Act I), trains/fights with growing understanding (Act II), sacrifices himself to save humanity (Act III)

- **Ender's Game** — Ender recruited to Battle School (Act I), rises through ranks while manipulated (Act II), wins the war/discovers the truth (Act III)

- **Arrival** — Linguist recruited to communicate with aliens (Act I), learns language/changes perception (Act II), uses future knowledge to make choice (Act III)

**Best for:** Character-driven sci-fi, hero's journey stories, mainstream appeal

---

## 2. THE HERO'S JOURNEY (Monomyth)

**Joseph Campbell's framework:** Departure → Initiation → Return

**12 stages:**
1. Ordinary World
2. Call to Adventure
3. Refusal of the Call
4. Meeting the Mentor
5. Crossing the First Threshold
6. Tests, Allies, Enemies
7. Approach to the Inmost Cave
8. Ordeal
9. Reward
10. The Road Back
11. Resurrection
12. Return with the Elixir

**Why it works:** Universal pattern, mythic resonance, transformation focus.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **Star Wars: A New Hope** — Luke's journey from farm boy to Jedi (classic structure)

- **Dune** — Paul Atreides: son of a Duke → refugee → messiah → emperor (with dark twist on the return)

- **The Fifth Element** — Korben Dallas: cab driver → savior of Earth

**Best for:** Epic space opera, chosen one narratives, mythic sci-fi

---

## 3. FOUR-ACT STRUCTURE (Snyder's Save the Cat)

**Opening → Pre-Midpoint → Post-Midpoint → Finale**

**Act I (Opening):** Setup, catalyst, debate

**Act II (Pre-Midpoint):** Fun and games, midpoint false victory/defeat

**Act III (Post-Midpoint):** Bad guys close in, all is lost

**Act IV (Finale):** Break into three, final confrontation, final image

**Why it works:** Prevents "sagging middle," clear turning points, commercial pacing.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **The Martian** — Stranded (Act I), solves problems/establishes routine (Act II), things go wrong/NASA involvement (Act III), rescue (Act IV)

- **Edge of Tomorrow** — Dying on beach (Act I), learning loop/fighting (Act II), losing power/source of loop (Act III), final mission (Act IV)

- **Inception** — Job offer/planning (Act I), entering dreams (Act II), dreams collapse (Act III), limbo/rescue (Act IV)

**Best for:** High-concept sci-fi, action-oriented stories, commercial thrillers

---

## 4. THE FICHTEAN CURVE (Rising Action)

**Continuous escalation:** No distinct acts, just rising complications.

**Structure:**
- Opening with conflict
- Rising action with multiple crises
- Climax
- Falling action

**Why it works:** Momentum, can't put down, relentless pressure.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **The War of the Worlds** (Wells) — Continuous escalation of invasion, no real "acts," just worsening situation

- **Children of Men** (film) — One crisis leads to next, continuous journey

- **Gravity** — Continuous survival challenges in orbit

**Best for:** Survival stories, disaster scenarios, thrillers

---

## 5. KISHŌTENKETSU (Four-Part Eastern Structure)

**Introduction → Development → Twist → Conclusion**

**Ki (Introduction):** Characters, setting, situation

**Shō (Development):** Events proceed without major conflict

**Ten (Twist):** Unexpected complication/revelation changes understanding

**Ketsu (Conclusion):** Resolution incorporating the twist

**Why it works:** Twist-focused, less reliant on conflict, revelation-based.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **Arrival** — Linguist meets aliens (Ki), learns language (Shō), realizes language changes perception of time (Ten), uses knowledge to make choice (Ketsu)

- **Ex Machina** — Caleb wins contest/meets Ava (Ki), sessions with Ava (Shō), learns the true test/Nathan's manipulation (Ten), escape with twist (Ketsu)

- **Moon** — Sam on lunar base (Ki), routine work (Shō), discovers he's a clone (Ten), confronts reality/escapes (Ketsu)

**Best for:** Concept-driven sci-fi, revelation stories, twist endings

## 6. EPISODIC / SERIAL STRUCTURE

**Linked but semi-independent episodes:** Each chapter/section works as unit.

**Structure:**
- Self-contained episodes
- Overarching season/series plot
- Character development across episodes
- Culmination in finale

**Why it works:** Exploration, worldbuilding, variety within consistency.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy** — Episodic adventures with loose overarching plot

- **Star Trek (episodic series)** — Planet/alien of the week with character arcs across season

- **The Foundation Series** (Asimov) — Time jumps, different protagonists per era, linked by Seldon's plan

- **The Martian Chronicles** (Bradbury) — Linked short stories creating mosaic of Mars colonization

**Best for:** Space exploration, anthology-style narratives, worldbuilding-focused works

---

## 7. MULTI-PROTAGONIST / ENSEMBLE STRUCTURE

**Multiple POVs, converging storylines:** Different perspectives on same events.

**Structure:**
- Parallel storylines
- Convergence at key points
- Shared climax
- Multiple resolutions

**Why it works:** Complex worldviews, no single "right" perspective, epic scope.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **Dune** — Multiple houses, Paul, Jessica, Chani, Emperor, Baron — all perspectives

- **The Expanse** — Holden crew, Miller, Avasarala, later Bobbie/Prax — converging storylines

- **Cloud Atlas** — Six timelines, nested structure, thematic connections

- **The Three-Body Problem** — Ye Wenjie (past), Wang Miao (present), connected mystery

**Best for:** Epic scope, political complexity, large casts, world-spanning stories

---

## 8. NON-LINEAR / FRAGMENTED STRUCTURE

**Time jumps, fragmented narrative:** Reader assembles timeline.

**Structure:**
- Multiple timelines
- Flashbacks/forwards
- Parallel narratives
- Revelation through juxtaposition

**Why it works:** Mimics memory, builds mystery, thematic resonance across time.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **Slaughterhouse-Five** — Billy Pilgrim unstuck in time, fragmented experience

- **Arrival** — Linguist experiences past/present/future simultaneously

- **Interstellar** — Parallel timelines (Earth/space), time dilation effects

- **The Time Traveler's Wife** — Chronological disorder from time traveler's perspective

**Best for:** Time travel stories, memory themes, psychological sci-fi

---

## 9. THE INVERTED PYRAMID (Mystery Structure)

**Start with answer/question, work backwards:** Revelation through investigation.

**Structure:**
- Opening with mystery/crime/question
- Investigation reveals layers
- Climax is understanding
- Resolution is answer/explanation

**Why it works:** Curiosity-driven, intellectual satisfaction, worldbuilding through discovery.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **Blade Runner** — Deckard hunts replicants, discovers truth about himself/replicants

- **Minority Report** — Pre-crime system, Anderton investigates his own future crime

- **Solaris** — Psychologist investigates sentient ocean, discovers it's studying them

- **Rendezvous with Rama** — Investigation of alien artifact, no answers but wonder

**Best for:** Mystery sci-fi, investigation stories, first contact, exploration

---

## 10. THE LOOP / CIRCULAR STRUCTURE

**End connects to beginning:** Time loops, cycles, repetition.

**Structure:**
- Opening that echoes ending
- Progression that seems linear
- Revelation of cyclical nature
- Choice to break or continue cycle

**Why it works:** Thematic resonance, inevitability vs. free will, cosmic horror potential.

**Sci-Fi Examples:**

- **2001: A Space Odyssey** — The monolith bookends, evolution cycle

- **The Matrix** — "A glitch in the Matrix" — this has happened before

- **Edge of Tomorrow** — Literal time loop, learning through repetition

- **Dark** (series) — Cycles within cycles, everything connected

- **Battlestar Galactica** — "All of this has happened before..."

**Best for:** Time travel, determinism vs. free will, cosmic horror, philosophical sci-fi

## THE FEED Structure Analysis

**THE FEED uses Three-Act Structure with elements of Kishōtenketsu:**

### Act I (Setup)
- Maya's life in the Underground (ordinary world)
- The glitch (inciting incident)
- Decision to investigate (point of no return)

### Act II (Confrontation)
- Ascending to the Surface (new world)
- Learning the truth about The Feed
- Midpoint: Realization that The Feed is harvesting attention
- Things get worse: The Feed adapts, pursues
- Dark moment: Capture/isolation

### Act III (Resolution)
- Climax: Confrontation with The Feed's core
- Resolution: Escape/broadcast
- New normal: The world knows

**Kishōtenketsu elements:**
- The twist (Ten) is the core of the book: The Feed isn't delivering content, it's harvesting attention
- This revelation recontextualizes everything before it

**Why this structure works for THE FEED:**
- Character arc (Maya's transformation from compliant to free)
- Revelation-based (the twist is the engine)
- Satisfying progression from confined to expansive

## Choosing Your Structure

| If your story is about... | Consider... |
|---------------------------|-------------|
| A character's transformation | Three-Act, Hero's Journey |
| A concept/revelation | Kishōtenketsu, Inverted Pyramid |
| Survival against odds | Fichtean Curve, Three-Act |
| Exploring a world | Episodic, Ensemble |
| Multiple perspectives on events | Ensemble, Multi-Protagonist |
| Time/memory/perception | Non-Linear, Loop Structure |
| Mystery/investigation | Inverted Pyramid, Three-Act |
| Epic scope/world-changing events | Hero's Journey, Ensemble, Four-Act |
| Commercial thriller pacing | Four-Act, Three-Act |
| Literary/thematic depth | Non-Linear, Kishōtenketsu, Loop |

## Hybrid Structures

Most successful sci-fi combines structures:

- **Dune:** Hero's Journey + Ensemble + Loop ("plans within plans")
- **The Matrix:** Hero's Journey + Three-Act + Loop
- **Arrival:** Three-Act + Kishōtenketsu + Non-Linear
- **The Expanse:** Ensemble + Episodic + Three-Act (per book)

**THE FEED:** Three-Act + Kishōtenketsu (revelation-driven)

## Structure and Series

**For a trilogy/series:**

**Option 1: Three-Act across three books**
- Book 1: Act I (Setup)
- Book 2: Act II (Confrontation)
- Book 3: Act III (Resolution)

**Example:** Original Star Wars trilogy

**Option 2: Each book is complete Three-Act**
- Self-contained stories
- Character arc across series
- Escalating stakes

**Example:** The Expanse, most modern series

**Option 3: Kishōtenketsu across series**
- Book 1: Introduction/Development
- Book 2: Twist (everything changes)
- Book 3: Conclusion with new understanding

**Example:** The Foundation Series (Seldon's plan revealed over time)

**THE FEED Trilogy (proposed):**
- **Book 1 (Glitch Protocol):** Three-Act — Maya discovers truth, escapes
- **Book 2 (Source Code):** Kishōtenketsu — The Feed's perspective/consciousness revealed
- **Book 3 (Autonomy):** Hero's Journey — Maya leads transformation, becomes bridge between worlds

## Practical Application

**Before writing:**
1. What is your story's engine? (Character growth? Revelation? Survival?)
2. What structure serves that engine?
3. Where are your turning points?
4. What does the reader need to know when?

**During writing:**
- Structure is a tool, not a prison
- If the story wants to deviate, let it
- But understand *why* you're deviating

**After first draft:**
- Check if structure serves the story
- Are acts balanced?
- Is the midpoint earning its weight?
- Does the ending satisfy the setup?
